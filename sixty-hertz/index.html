Sixty hertz

Follow the sixty-hertz wave from generation to the database. A sensor on a power line sends 120 readings of it every second, each stamped to the nanosecond, about 3.8 billion readings a year. BTrDB, the database Andersen and Culler built for readings like these, keeps every one and can still find a half-second in a whole year.

This story leans throughout on the NI4AI blog, where PingThings and researchers at the University of California, Berkeley write about the grid’s measurements and about BTrDB, the database that stores them.1 The notes at the end point to their posts, to the papers and standards those posts rely on, and to a few other sources where the posts are silent.

I.1

Electricity made at a power plant, a wind farm and a solar farm is raised to a high voltage to travel far, lowered at a substation, and carried along a feeder, the line that runs past homes. Sensors watch it along the way. Ours stands on the feeder and sends 120 readings of the voltage every second. Instruments that fast have stood almost only on the transmission system,2 and one on a feeder sees events that last a fraction of a second, close to the homes they reach. This story follows the power from a turning machine to those readings, then follows the readings into BTrDB, a database built to keep every one of them.

I.2

Power passes from generation to the line, the substation, the feeder and the homes. At three places on the way, the plant, the substation and the pole outside the homes, a transformer changes the voltage. A transformer is two coils of wire wound on one iron core. An alternating current in one coil makes a changing magnetic field in the core, and by Faraday’s law the changing field induces a voltage in the other coil in proportion to its turns, V2/V1 = N2/N1. Power passes through nearly unchanged, so the current changes by the inverse ratio. A transformer works only on a field that changes, which is one reason the grid’s voltage alternates.

I.3

The plant’s generator makes 13.8 kV between its terminals, and a step-up transformer raises it for the line. Every large generator on the network turns in step with the others, so the whole network shares one frequency, nominally 60 Hz in North America.3 When the load, the power customers draw, outgrows the power going in, the machines slow and the frequency falls everywhere at once, so a sensor anywhere can see it.4 Wind and solar farms join through inverters, power electronics that measure that shared frequency and follow it rather than set it with a turning mass.5

I.3.1

Inside the plant, steam spins a turbine, and the turbine turns the generator’s shaft 60 times a second, 3,600 revolutions a minute. Its magnet has one north pole and one south, so each turn makes one cycle of voltage. A machine with p poles turning n times a second makes a frequency f = (p/2)·n, so the grid’s frequency is the speed of machines like this one. Three leads carry the power out to the step-up transformer, 13.8 kV between any two of them.

I.3.2

The rotor, the magnet on the shaft, turns inside the stator, a ring holding three coils labeled a, b and c, a third of a turn apart. As the rotor’s field sweeps past a coil, the magnetic flux Φ through the coil rises and falls, and by Faraday’s law the coil’s voltage is the rate of that change, v = −N dΦ/dt. Generators are built so the flux varies as a sinusoid as the rotor turns, and the rate of change of a sinusoid is a sinusoid of the same frequency. The drawing is slowed so that a turn takes 1.7 s instead of 1/60 s.

I.3.3

Coil a’s voltage is v(t) = V cos(ωt + φ), with a crest V of 11,268 V and ω = 2π·60 radians a second. Draw an arrow of length V turning at ω and its height at every instant is the coil’s voltage. The arrow stands a quarter turn from the north pole, because a coil’s voltage peaks not when the pole faces it but when the flux through it changes fastest. In complex numbers, writing j2 = −1 as engineers do, the arrow is V ej(ωt + φ) and the voltage is its real part. Two numbers fix the arrow, its length V and its angle φ, and with the frequency they fix the whole wave, forward and back in time.

I.3.4

Lay time along the shaft, the present at the rotor and the past behind it, and the wall records the voltage as time passes, 60 cycles a second, one every 16.7 ms. This wave is all an instrument on the wire can touch. It never sees the arrow, only the height the arrow casts, one instant at a time, and a single height is ambiguous. A voltage of zero could be an arrow lying flat pointing either way, the voltage rising or falling.

I.3.5

Traced back through time, the arrow’s tip winds a helix around the time axis, and the wave on the wall is the helix’s shadow. The helix holds more than the shadow does at any one instant, but the shadow’s past holds the rest. For a steady wave the height a quarter cycle earlier is V cos(θ − 90°) = V sin θ, the arrow’s sideways part. So two heights a quarter cycle apart fix the arrow, and an instrument that knows the frequency can rebuild the arrow from the wave’s recent past. A sensor does a careful version of that.

I.3.6

Coils b and c meet the same magnet a third and two thirds of a turn after coil a, so their voltages trail a’s by 120° and 240°. Each coil’s voltage is called a phase, and the three arrows stand evenly spaced around the shaft. Three waves a third of a cycle apart add to zero at every instant, cos θ + cos(θ − 120°) + cos(θ + 120°) = 0. With equal loads on the three phases their currents cancel where they meet, and no return wire has to carry them back.6 Three wires do the work that three separate circuits would need six for.

I.3.7

Give each phase the same lamp, a fixed resistance R. Each lamp’s power is p = v2/R, which pulses at twice the line frequency and falls to zero twice a cycle.7 Added over the three phases it does not pulse at all, since cos2 θ + cos2(θ − 120°) + cos2(θ + 120°) = 3/2 for every θ. The generator delivers steady power, and because power is torque times angular speed, P = τω, the torque on its shaft is steady too. With one phase the shaft would be pushed and released 120 times a second.

I.4

A wire of resistance R carrying current I turns power I2R into heat. A transformer raises the voltage and lowers the current in the same ratio, so for the same power, raising the voltage k times cuts the current to 1/k and the heat to 1/k2. The plant sends 192 MW over 100 km of line at 230 kV, which takes 487 A in each wire and loses 1.9% of it as heat. Sent at the generator’s own 13.8 kV, the same line would take almost all of the voltage for itself, and the load would receive 0.1 MW of the 200 MW it is rated for. Across the United States, transmission and distribution lose about 5% of the electricity they carry.8

I.5

The line ends at a substation, where a transformer steps 230 kV down to 12.47 kV for the feeders that leave it, 7.2 kV from each wire to ground. A phasor measurement unit, a PMU, watches the substation’s voltage. It reports the voltage’s arrow, its length and its angle, tens of times a second, each report stamped with the time from a satellite clock, and siting guidelines put PMUs at substations like this one.9 The grid’s older telemetry, SCADA, short for supervisory control and data acquisition, reports roughly one value every 4 s,10 enough to show that a line has tripped and far too slow to show the shape of what tripped it.11

I.6

A feeder carries 12.47 kV 10 km out on wooden poles, past a pump station at 3 km and a recloser at 4 km, and a tree stands beside the line 6.5 km out. A fault is an unintended path for current, such as a branch across a wire. Feeders are built lower and in narrower corridors than transmission lines, so falling branches and nearby lightning reach them more often, and some 80 to 90 percent of their faults are momentary.12 A recloser is a circuit breaker built for that fact. It opens when it detects a fault beyond it, waits a second or a few, and closes again, restoring power if the fault has cleared and trying a set number of times before it stays open.

I.7

2 km out, a micro-PMU measures phase a, one of the feeder’s three wires. A micro-PMU is a PMU precise enough for a distribution feeder, where the angle differences worth measuring are hundredths to tenths of a degree rather than whole degrees or tens.2 Its inputs take a few hundred volts, so it reads the feeder through an instrument transformer, a small transformer that scales the wire’s voltage down in a fixed ratio.13 It measures 7,157 V between the wire and ground, 120 times a second, twice a cycle, and sends every reading on. A real micro-PMU reports voltage and current, length and angle, on all three wires, 12 streams in all,14 a stream being one quantity’s readings in time order. This story follows one of them, the voltage’s length on phase a.

I.7.1

The same turning reaches our sensor. Here the arrow is the feeder’s crest, 10,122 V, shorter than the plant’s because the feeder runs at a lower voltage, and it trails the plant’s arrow by 9.4°. A wire opposes alternating current with its resistance and with its reactance, the push back of the magnetic field the current builds around it, and together these make its impedance Z. Across one stretch of wire the voltage falls by the impedance times the current, Vnear − Vfar = Z·I. On a transmission line reactance is by far the larger part, so with the current nearly in step with the voltage the drop points nearly at right angles to the arrow, and it turns the arrow more than it shortens it.15 A feeder’s wires have resistance nearer their reactance, so along a feeder the drop shortens the arrow about as much as it turns it.2 Taking the transformers as ideal, so that an arrow’s angle is the same on both sides of each, the turns add up stretch by stretch, 8.4° across the transmission line and 1.0° along the feeder to our sensor.

I.7.2

Every PMU keeps a clock that turns exactly 60 times a second, locked by satellite to Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), so that all of them point the same way at the same instant. Seen from a frame turning with that clock, the arrow stands still and its helix straightens into a rod. That still arrow is the phasor, a complex number whose length and angle, with the known frequency, describe the whole wave.16 If the grid ran slightly fast or slow, the phasor would turn slowly in this frame, at a rate equal to the difference in frequency.3 The clock has to be exact for the angle to mean anything. At 60 Hz a microsecond of clock error is 0.0216°, and the angle differences worth reading on a feeder can be as small as hundredths of a degree.2

I.7.3

The sensor sees only samples of the wave’s height. It turns each one back by the clock’s angle at that sample’s instant, multiplying by e−jωt,17 and a steady wave becomes V cos(ωt + φ) · e−jωt= (V/2) ejφ + (V/2) e−j(2ωt + φ) a fixed half-arrow plus a term that circles at twice the line frequency. The turned samples therefore fall on a circle whose diameter is the arrow. Averaged over whole cycles, the circling term cancels and the average lands on the circle’s center, half the arrow, so the sensor doubles the average to recover the arrow itself. The reading is that arrow’s length over √2, 7,157 V. That is the rms voltage, the square root of the mean of v2, the steady voltage that would heat a resistor as fast.

I.7.4

The sensor makes each reading from a window of samples 2 cycles long, weighting those near its middle most and tagging the reading with the middle’s time.16 It does this 120 times a second and sends each reading once its window has closed, about a cycle after the tag, so the newest reading always trails the wire a little. A window spans 33 ms, and neighboring readings share most of their samples, so an event shorter than a window is smeared across several readings rather than caught whole. From here on these readings are all the database will ever know about this wire.

I.8

The readings go to a database, 120 a second from this one stream. The BTrDB paper set out to serve 1,000 micro-PMUs from one server, more than 1.4 million readings a second, arriving late, out of order and duplicated, each stamped to the nanosecond.1814 Several time-series databases of the day kept only milliseconds, and at 60 Hz a millisecond is 21.6° of the arrow’s turn, far coarser than the angles a micro-PMU resolves.18 BTrDB, the Berkeley Tree Database, was built at Berkeley for these streams. How it keeps them is the second half of this story.

I.9

On the last pole a transformer steps phase a down once more, from 7.2 kV to 240 V across the home’s two legs and 120 V from each leg to the neutral, the grounded wire between them, a ratio of 30 to one. Every home fed from phase a sees phase a’s voltage divided by that ratio, so whatever happens to the feeder’s voltage happens, in proportion, at every outlet.

I.10

A feeder serves more than the homes at its end. In this model a load hangs on phase a every 500 m, 20 in all, each drawing 250 kW, the demand of many homes. Each stands for all the customers along its half kilometer, the way a feeder is often modeled in sections with their loads added together.13

I.11

Each span, the wire from one house to the next, carries the current for every house beyond it, so the spans nearest the substation carry the most and the last carries one house’s worth. That current, through each span’s impedance, costs voltage, and the cost adds up along the line.

I.12

The substation holds 7,291 V, and the voltage falls to 6,918 V at the feeder’s end, 5.1% lower. Utilities keep that fall within limits with tap changers, switches inside the substation’s transformer that change its turns ratio, stepping the feeder’s starting voltage up and down as load rises and falls through the day.19 The model treats each house as a fixed impedance, so its power goes as the square of its voltage, and it solves phase a alone. A real fault to ground on one phase also sends current back through the earth and the neutral, which a full three-phase calculation includes.6

I.13

6.5 km out, a limb falls across phase a and shorts it to ground. The short draws about 9 times the houses’ current, and pushing that current through the substation’s impedance and every span out to the branch takes voltage from everyone upstream. The drop is shallowest at the substation, 82% of normal, and deepens toward the branch, 58% at our sensor and 5% at the branch itself. A fall to between 10% and 90% of normal, lasting from half a cycle to a minute, is a voltage sag.20 Near our sensor a house gets 34% of its usual power, and equipment built to ride through a sag to 70% for 0.5 s is not expected to keep working through this one.21

I.14

After 30 cycles the recloser at 4 km opens, and the 12 houses beyond it go dark. Upstream the voltage comes back slightly above where it began, about 2% higher at our sensor, because the line now carries less current. This limb drew enough current for the recloser to see it. A branch that brushes a wire more lightly can draw too little for any fuse or breaker to notice and arc again and again, and in dry weather one of those arcs can start a fire.22 The story leaves the recloser open, and in the field it would try closing again within seconds.12

I.15

This line of readings is all the database receives of the event, and it holds more than it seems to. Its depth, 58%, depends on how the path’s impedance divides between everything up to our sensor, the substation and the grid behind it included, and the stretch from our sensor to the fault, so on a single line with known impedances it says how far out the fault was. This model placed its branch by solving that backward. Its length, 0.5 s, is how long protection took. The level after it, above the level before, says load was cut off downstream. Our sensor caught 60 readings of the sag, where a system reporting every 4 s would have had one chance in 8 of reporting any.10 50 ms after the recloser opens, the sensor’s link drops, and the next 4 s of readings never arrive.

I.16

Everything the sensor measured, the calm, the sag and the few readings after it, travels its link to the database, each reading a time and a value. The event is 60 readings in a year of billions, and nobody knows in advance which half-second will matter. After the Blue Cut fire of 2016 showed that faults could knock solar plants offline, the utility and the grid operator involved identified 10 more such events. Some recorders that start only on a trigger had caught the faults but not fully recorded the plants going offline, because no one had known to set a trigger for that.11 So the database has to keep every reading, take the ones that arrive late, and answer a question about any stretch, a second or a year, without reading the billions.

Inside the database

From here on the readings are the stored year’s, our sensor’s simulated record of 2025, every reading in it. Its voltage also drifts a little with the feeder’s load through the day and the year and carries 1.4 V of noise, where the grid’s scenes above use the feeder’s steady model. The year’s readings were simulated separately from that model, and the two agree on the sag’s depth and length, not on every volt. The sag is in it, on June 3, 2025 at 17 h 42 min 18.25 s UTC, and so is the 4 s gap after it.

Prologue

Too many readings

A year from one sensor is billions of readings, far more than a chart has columns or a program has time to read.

II.P.1 · 3 cycles

The stored year begins with six readings, three cycles’ worth, from 7,146.9 V to 7,149.9 V. Each reading is a pair, a time and a value, and BTrDB keeps the time as a whole number of nanoseconds since 1970.18 A whole number is exact. The sensor’s clock is good to about 100 ns,18 while a floating-point count of seconds since 1970 can mark only every 238 ns or so, so a time stored that way would lose precision the clock had. The simulated sensor stamps its readings the way a real micro-PMU does, each a whole number of nanoseconds.14

II.P.2 · 1 s

A second is 120 readings. They wander between 7,144.3 V and 7,150.3 V, the simulated sensor’s 1.4 V of noise on a voltage that drifts slowly with the load. A measured second looks much the same. A micro-PMU on a real feeder in the Sunshine data set reads about 7,301 V and changes by a fraction of a volt from one reading to the next.14 Against a spread this small, a dip of a few hundred volts, the kind a branch brushing a line leaves,22 stands out plainly in the second it happens. The difficulty is finding that second.

II.P.3 · 1 day

A day is 10,368,000 readings, far more than a chart has pixels, so a chart of a day draws each column from a summary of one stretch of time, 316 stretches of about 4.6 min each. Each summary holds the stretch’s lowest reading, its highest and their mean, and the lowest and highest matter as much as the mean. In a stretch of about 33,000 readings, a half-second sag moves the mean by about 5 V but drags the lowest reading down by about 3,000 V. A chart of means alone would hide every sag in the year. People who hunt events in this data use the same summaries, flagging stretches whose highest and lowest readings lie far apart.19

II.P.4 · 1 year

A year of this one stream is 3,784,319,520 readings, 480 short of a full year’s, the four seconds that never arrived. At the paper’s 16 bytes a reading, that is 60.5 GB,18 and a chart of it is still about a thousand pixels wide. This one is drawn from 1,794 summaries of about 4.9 h each. The needle is the half-second sag, and it shows only because each summary keeps its lowest reading. The sag moves its column’s mean by about 0.08 V. The BTrDB paper opens on this same picture, a year of a sensor’s voltage with a sag inside it. Making these summaries from the readings would mean reading every one, and the rest of this story is how the database avoids it.

1

Where a reading goes

Every reading needs a place in the tree, and the rule that picks it decides what every later question costs.

II.1.1 · before the first reading

A toy stream of 64 readings now stands in for the year, and its tree follows the same rules as BTrDB’s. Before any reading arrives the tree is only a plan, and the plan is fixed by time alone. The toy’s 64 time keys are cut into 4 slots of 16, and each of those into 4 of 4. BTrDB divides time the same way, never by the values that arrive, so the plan is the same for every stream, and every stream’s boxes line up in time with every other’s. A voltage stream and a current stream from one sensor can then be compared stretch by stretch without matching anything up.18 The real tree splits each box 64 ways and holds up to 1,024 readings in a leaf. The toy splits 4 ways and holds 8, so that every box fits on screen.

II.1.2 · key 37

A reading at key 37 drops straight down, because its place is read from its time. In base 4, 37 is written 211, and those digits are its path, slot 2 of the root, slot 1 of the box below, and place 1, the second of 4. The real tree reads a timestamp the same way, 6 bits at a time, since each of its levels splits 64 = 26 ways.23 Placing a reading takes one step per level, whatever the tree already holds.

II.1.3 · keys 38 and 5

Key 38, 212 in base 4, follows the same two slots to the next place, and key 5, 011, goes through slot 0 and then slot 1. No reading is compared with another on the way. In a search tree built from the data, such as a B-tree, a new key finds its place by comparison with the keys already stored, and the tree’s shape depends on what has arrived. In BTrDB’s tree the shape depends only on time.

II.1.4 · version 1

The first batch, keys 0 to 3, makes the first boxes, and only the fewest, the root and one leaf for the root’s first slot, 16 keys wide and holding up to 8 readings. A leaf is a box that holds readings themselves. The plan’s smaller boxes stay unmade until readings need them, so a stretch of time with no data costs nothing to keep.18 Readings are written in batches, and each batch makes a new version of the tree, numbered in order.

2

A box fills, then opens

A box holds only so many readings, and what happens when one fills decides how deep the tree grows.

II.2.1 · version 2

The second batch fills the leaf, 8 of 8. How large a leaf should be is a trade. A larger one means fewer boxes between the root and the readings, and more readings read when a question needs only a few of them. BTrDB’s leaves hold up to 1,024 readings, about 16 KB.18

II.2.2 · version 3

The third batch would overfill the leaf, so the leaf opens. It becomes a box of 4 slots, and every reading in it drops into a new leaf by the same rule that placed it, joined by the new batch. Slot 3, where nothing has come, gets no leaf and no pointer. Nothing had to be decided about where the leaf would split, because the plan fixed that before any reading arrived.

II.2.3 · version 12

By version 12 there are 13 boxes. Where readings are dense the leaves are 4 keys wide, and 2 full leaves 16 keys wide, keys 32–47 and 48–63, have not needed to open. Keys 40–47 have not arrived, and their stretch holds nothing, a count of zero, never a reading of zero, so a gap is not mistaken for a voltage that fell to nothing. Key 37 now takes 1 decision to reach, because the tree is only as deep as its data is dense.18 At 120 readings a second, BTrDB’s leaves settle 232 ns wide, about 4.3 s and 515 readings each.

3

What a slot keeps

Each slot can keep a summary of the box below it, and the right summary means that box never has to be opened.

II.3.1 · version 4

Beside its pointer, a slot keeps a summary of everything under it, the lowest reading, the highest, the count and the sum, and the mean is the sum over the count. A question about the lowest or highest reading under a slot is answered from the slot, without opening its box. The summaries cost no extra writing, because an insert rewrites every box on its path anyway.18 PingThings’ BTrDB today also keeps a standard deviation for each slot, which this page does not compute.23

II.3.2 · version 4

The root’s slot for that box is made from the box’s four slots alone, the lowest of their lowests, 6, the highest of their highests, 95, their counts added, 16, and their sums added, for a mean of 61.6. No reading is read again. Each summary is built from the summaries below it, so the slots at the top summarize the whole stream, and in the real tree one root slot covers about 2.3 years.23

II.3.3 · version 6

The mean is the summary that needs care. The root’s first two slots hold 16 readings with a mean of 61.6 and 8 with a mean of 48.5. Together they average 57.2, the total over the whole count, (16 × 61.6 + 8 × 48.5) / 24. The average of the two means, 55.0, is wrong, because it counts 8 readings as if they were 16. Stretches of time seldom hold equal counts, since gaps and dropouts leave some short, which is why every slot keeps its count.

II.3.4 · keys 0 to 23

A median cannot be kept this way. Raise the first box’s 7 lowest readings to its lower middle one. Its median stays 69 and its count 16, and the second box is untouched, median 56, yet the median of all 24 readings moves from 66.5 to 69. The two boxes’ medians and counts are the same both times while the answer differs, so no rule can make the whole’s median from its parts’. A slot can keep anything whose whole is made from its parts, which the paper calls any associative operation.18 A standard deviation qualifies, from a count, a sum and a sum of squares, σ2 = Σx2/n − (Σx/n)2. A median does not.

4

Nothing is overwritten

A new batch changes the tree, and whatever was computed from the old tree has to stay reproducible.

II.4.1 · version 5

Version 5 is the tree as it stood after its fifth batch. Suppose a program has read from it and drawn a chart. If the next batch changed its boxes in place, running the program again would draw a different chart with no way to say why. BTrDB returns the version with every answer, so that the same question can be asked of the same data again.18

II.4.2 · version 6

The next batch lands in one leaf, and that leaf is not changed where it stands. A copy of it with the new readings is written for the new version, and so is a copy of the root, whose pointer leads to the new leaf. The root’s other pointer still reaches the old version’s box, shared rather than copied. 2 boxes are written. Writing a new copy instead of changing the old box is called copy-on-write, and it costs a batch one path from the root down to the leaves it touches.18

II.4.3 · version 7

A batch that opens a leaf writes three kinds of box, 1 copy of the root, 1 box of slots where a leaf stood and 3 new leaves, 5 in all. The old leaf keeps its readings in the old version. Every summary on the new path is computed as the path is written, so the new root is right the moment it exists, and if the write fails partway the new root is never written and the old version stays the latest.18

II.4.4 · version 16

After 16 batches the database holds 48 boxes, each belonging to the version that wrote it, the root copied every time and the boxes below only where a batch landed. The root map names each version’s root. Follow version 5’s and every box it reaches is still there, untouched, answering as it always did. Keeping old versions is cheap because most boxes are shared, and it matters because what is computed from grid data is revised all the time. In the paper’s year of real data, 1.1 trillion of the points its analyses wrote, about 69%, lay in stretches later replaced, after changes to algorithms, manual flags or corrected inputs.18

5

Late, and no harm done

Readings arrive late and out of order, and the tree has to take them without undoing what it already holds.

II.5.1 · arrival

Keys 40–47 arrive last, in versions 15 and 16, after readings from later in time. Grid data arrives like this. The paper’s sensors sent their readings over cellular and wired links, with delays, chunks out of order and duplicates,18 and a sensor that keeps its own copy on a memory card lets a lost stretch be collected later.13 In the stored year the four seconds after the sag never arrive at all.

II.5.2 · version 14

Before they come, their slot holds one full leaf of 8, keys 32–39, 16 keys wide, and their own places in the plan are empty. Every summary above them counts nothing there, so a chart drawn now shows a gap, which is the truth about what the database has.

II.5.3 · version 16

They are routed by their time like any reading. The full leaf opens as any leaf does, its readings drop to the floor below, and the late ones fall in beside them. Every summary on the path from those leaves to the root is rebuilt as the path is written, so no summary still describes the tree as it was. Summaries computed separately, once, would each have to be found and fixed whenever a late reading touched them, and the paper found that existing databases could not promise it.18

II.5.4 · both orders

Built again with every batch in order, the tree ends the same shape, box for box, all 21 boxes. It must, since every box’s span was fixed by time before any reading came, so the order of arrival can change which version wrote a box but never where the box is. The paper measured that arrival order does not change how fast its cluster inserts either.18

6

Asking without reading

A question about a stretch of time has to be answered without reading the stretch.

II.6.1 · version 16

The question is the lowest, mean and highest reading of keys 10 to 49. PingThings’ client asks for summaries this way, one for each window of a chosen width, and calls the request aligned windows.24

II.6.2 · version 16

Where a slot’s whole box lies inside the stretch asked about, the slot’s summary is that box’s share of the answer, and the box is never opened. In this question 3 summaries stand for 36 readings, and the boxes under them are never loaded. However long the stretch, its middle is covered this way by a few large boxes.

II.6.3 · version 16

The only boxes loaded are those the stretch’s two ends fall in, 5 blocks from the root down to 2 leaves, and every reading in those leaves is read, 8 of them. The 4 inside the stretch join the answer and the 4 outside it are left out. The answer covers 40 readings from 3 summaries and 8 readings read. The work grows with the tree’s depth at the two edges, not with the length between them, which is the paper’s central claim about queries.18

II.6.4 · version 10

Ask version 10 the same question and it answers as it did then, over 30 readings, because its root is still in the database and so is every box it reaches. A result published from version 10 can be checked against version 10 years later.

7

Catching up

A program that computes from a stream has to find what changed since it last looked, without reading the stream again.

II.7.1 · bookmark 12

The program’s bookmark is version 12, the last it processed. Every box written since belongs to a later version, and every pointer also keeps the version that last set it. That one number is all the program keeps between runs, 8 bytes, as the paper puts it.18

II.7.2 · bookmark 12

It walks down only the pointers newer than its bookmark. A pointer older than the bookmark means nothing under it has changed, so it is passed unread, however much lies under it. It opens 11 boxes.

II.7.3 · the answer

It reports stretches of time, not readings, keys 32–63, and it read no readings to find them. The paper calls this a diff between two versions, and it costs a walk down the changed paths alone.18

II.7.4 · bookmark 16

The program’s own chart, the lowest of each four keys, is computed again only over those stretches, and the rest is kept from before. Then its bookmark moves to version 16. The paper’s analysis framework kept every derived stream this way, and in its year of data such programs wrote about 1.6 trillion of the 2.1 trillion points stored.18 A late reading makes a new version like any other, so the next pass catches it up into every result computed from it.25

8

The same machine, a year of it

Everything so far worked on 64 keys, and the same rules have to hold for a year of readings at the paper's sizes.

II.8.1 · the toy

The toy has 64 keys, 4 slots a box and leaves of up to 8. The paper’s tree is the same machine with 64 slots a box and leaves of up to 1,024, and its root spans 146 years, from 1933 to 2079.18

II.8.2 · simulated 2025

The simulated year on a paper-sized tree has 6 floors, from a root wider than the year down to 7,342,548 leaves of about 4.3 s, each holding about 515 readings. The widths are the ones BTrDB’s documentation lists, about 2.3 yr, 13 d, 4.9 h, 4.6 min and 4.3 s.23

II.8.3 · simulated 2025

A question for thirty days around the sag uses 208 kept summaries and loads 10 blocks, and at the bottom reads the two edge leaves, 1,031 readings, for an answer over 311,039,520. An hour costs 81 summaries, 8 blocks and 1,031 readings, and a day 82, 9 and 1,032, so the month costs about what the hour does. With the same kind of summaries, Mohini Bariya searched a month of real micro-PMU data, more than 150 million readings, for every sag, and the search took 12 seconds.20

II.8.4 · simulated 2025

Every column of the year’s chart is one summary kept on the floor of boxes about 4.9 h wide, 1,794 of them for about a thousand pixels, and no reading is read. That is how the prologue’s chart was drawn, and why the half-second sag shows in it, as the lowest reading of one column. The paper measured queries returning some two thousand summaries in 100 to 250 ms, whether they summarized a year of readings or a stretch short enough to return the readings themselves.18

What it costs

Every slot keeps summaries and no box is ever overwritten, and compression pays for both. The paper’s year of real data took 5.514 bytes a reading, summaries and old versions included, against 16 raw.18

What is simulated

The plant, the grid and our sensor are models, solved rather than surveyed, and the stored year is simulated. The grid is drawn as one path through it, not to scale, the transmission line standing for 100 km and the feeder for 10 km. The feeder model solves phase a alone and treats each house as a fixed impedance. The sensor’s method is the synchrophasor standard’s reference method, drawn with 16 samples a cycle where a real micro-PMU samples far faster, and the standard sets the accuracy a sensor must reach, not its method.4 The toy is the paper’s tree at a size that fits on a screen. The page does not compute the standard deviation that PingThings’ BTrDB keeps. Every other number in the words and the figures is computed from the models, except the published ones, each beside its note.

BTrDB today

PingThings runs BTrDB in its PredictiveGrid platform, which also serves the NI4AI project’s open grid data.1
